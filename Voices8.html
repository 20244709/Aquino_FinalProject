Understand mental health / Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia

What schizophrenia is, the early signs to look out for, and how to support someone who is living with it.

Schizophrenia is a serious mental health condition that affects how a person thinks, feels, and perceives reality. It usually begins in late adolescence or early adulthood, and with the right treatment and support many people manage their symptoms and live meaningful lives.

Three groups of symptoms

Symptoms of schizophrenia are usually described in three groups.

  • Positive symptoms: Experiences added to a person's usual perception, such as hallucinations (hearing or seeing things others do not), delusions (strongly held false beliefs), and disorganised thinking or speech.
  • Negative symptoms: A reduction in usual abilities or feelings, such as reduced emotional expression, low motivation, speaking less, and withdrawing from social activities.
  • Cognitive symptoms: Difficulties with attention, memory, and planning that can make work, study, and everyday tasks harder.

Early warning signs

Before a first episode of psychosis there are often gradual changes over weeks or months. Spotting them early can lead to earlier support.

Changes in behaviour

  • Withdrawing from friends, family and activities
  • A drop in performance at school or work
  • Neglecting personal hygiene or appearance
  • Sleeping much more or much less than usual

Changes in thinking and perception

  • Trouble concentrating or thinking clearly
  • Feeling suspicious or uneasy around others
  • Unusual beliefs or ideas that seem out of character
  • Hearing, seeing or sensing things others don't

Changes in emotion

  • Seeming flat or showing less emotion
  • Strong emotions that don't match the situation

Getting treatment early for psychosis is linked to better long-term outcomes.

Having some of these signs doesn't mean someone has this condition. Only a qualified professional can make a diagnosis.

How to help

You don't need to have all the answers. Being patient, kind and steady can make a real difference.

Things that help

  • Stay calm, and speak clearly and simply.
  • Acknowledge how they feel, even if you don't share their experience: "That sounds really frightening."
  • Encourage them to keep up with treatment and appointments.
  • Help with practical things like routines, meals and getting to appointments.
  • Remember the person behind the condition, and keep including them.

Things to avoid

  • Arguing about whether hallucinations or delusions are real.
  • Pretending to agree with beliefs you don't share.
  • Using hurtful words like "crazy" or "psycho".
  • Assuming they're dangerous. People with schizophrenia are more likely to be harmed by others than to harm anyone.

When to get professional help

Reach out to a professional, for yourself or someone you care about, if:

  • You notice several early warning signs, especially together
  • Someone is hearing or seeing things others don't, or holding unusual beliefs
  • They're struggling to look after themselves day to day
  • They've stopped medication without medical advice
  • There's any risk of harm to themselves or others (get help straight away)

A good first step is to talk to a doctor, psychologist, psychiatrist, or your school or workplace guidance counsellor. You can also browse support resources.

If someone is in immediate danger or at risk of harming themselves, don't wait: get urgent support now.

Look after yourself too. Supporting someone can be tiring. Make time to rest, set limits on what you can give, and lean on your own support network.
