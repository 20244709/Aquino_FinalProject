Understand mental health / Bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder

What bipolar disorder is, the signs to look out for, and how to support someone who is living with it.

Bipolar disorder is a chronic mental health condition marked by extreme shifts in mood, energy, activity levels, and concentration. These shifts involve episodes of elevated mood (mania or hypomania) and episodes of low mood (depression), often with periods of steadier mood in between. With ongoing treatment, many people manage their symptoms well.

Types of bipolar disorder

There are three main types, depending on the pattern and intensity of mood episodes.

  • Bipolar I: Defined by at least one manic episode lasting at least seven days (or any duration if hospitalisation is required). Major depressive or hypomanic episodes often occur, though they are not required for diagnosis.
  • Bipolar II: Defined by at least one hypomanic episode (lasting at least four days) and at least one major depressive episode (lasting at least two weeks), with no history of full mania.
  • Cyclothymia: Involves numerous periods of hypomanic symptoms and depressive symptoms that do not meet full episode criteria. These shifts occur for at least two years in adults (one year in children and adolescents) without symptom-free stretches longer than two months.

Signs and symptoms

Signs depend on which kind of mood episode someone is having.

During mania or hypomania

  • Feeling unusually high, euphoric or irritable
  • Lots of energy while needing much less sleep
  • Talking fast and jumping between ideas
  • Feeling unusually important or powerful
  • Taking risks, such as overspending or reckless driving

During depression

  • Persistent sadness, emptiness or hopelessness
  • Very low energy and loss of interest in activities
  • Sleeping too much or too little
  • Trouble concentrating or making decisions
  • Thoughts of death or suicide

Mania can feel good to the person experiencing it, so they may not see it as a problem. Some people also have episodes with features of both mania and depression at the same time.

Having some of these signs doesn't mean someone has this condition. Only a qualified professional can make a diagnosis.

How to help

You don't need to have all the answers. Being patient, kind and steady can make a real difference.

Things that help

  • Learn about the condition and the person's own early warning signs.
  • When they're well, agree a plan together for what to do if an episode starts.
  • Encourage regular routines for sleep, meals and activity.
  • Support them to keep up with treatment, even when they feel well.
  • Stay patient. Mood episodes are part of the condition, not a choice.

Things to avoid

  • Blaming them for things they said or did during an episode.
  • Calling everyday mood changes "bipolar".
  • Arguing with them during a manic episode. Wait for a calmer moment.
  • Encouraging them to stop treatment without talking to their doctor.

When to get professional help

Reach out to a professional, for yourself or someone you care about, if:

  • Mood swings are affecting daily life or relationships
  • Several days of very little sleep with lots of energy
  • Risky or out-of-character behaviour
  • Low mood that has lasted two weeks or more
  • Any thoughts of self-harm or suicide (get help straight away)

A good first step is to talk to a doctor, psychologist, psychiatrist, or your school or workplace guidance counsellor. You can also browse support resources.

If someone is in immediate danger or at risk of harming themselves, don't wait: get urgent support now.

Look after yourself too. Supporting someone can be tiring. Make time to rest, set limits on what you can give, and lean on your own support network.
