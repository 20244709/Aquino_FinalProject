Understand mental health / Trauma

Trauma

What trauma is, the signs to look out for, and how to support someone who has been through it.

Trauma is the emotional and physical response to a deeply distressing or frightening event, such as violence, an accident, a disaster, or loss. Many people recover with time and support, but for some the effects persist and develop into a trauma-related condition.

Trauma-related conditions

Not everyone who goes through trauma develops a condition. When symptoms are intense or don't ease, they may fall into one of these.

  • Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD): Involves intrusive memories or flashbacks, avoiding reminders of the event, negative changes in mood and thinking, and feeling on edge, lasting longer than one month.
  • Acute stress disorder: Similar symptoms to PTSD that appear within the first month after a traumatic event and last from three days to one month.
  • Complex PTSD: Can follow prolonged or repeated trauma. Along with PTSD symptoms, it involves difficulty managing emotions, a persistently negative sense of self, and trouble in relationships.
  • Adjustment disorder: Emotional or behavioural difficulties that develop in response to a stressful life change and are stronger than would usually be expected.

Signs and symptoms

Reactions to trauma can start straight away or appear months later.

Reliving the event

  • Flashbacks or intrusive memories
  • Nightmares
  • Strong distress or physical reactions to reminders

Avoidance and mood

  • Avoiding people, places or conversations linked to the event
  • Feeling numb, detached or cut off from others
  • Persistent fear, guilt, shame or anger
  • Losing interest in activities

Feeling on edge

  • Being easily startled or constantly alert for danger
  • Trouble sleeping or concentrating
  • Irritability or angry outbursts

Children may show trauma differently, for example through play, clinginess, or changes in sleep or behaviour.

Having some of these signs doesn't mean someone has this condition. Only a qualified professional can make a diagnosis.

How to help

You don't need to have all the answers. Being patient, kind and steady can make a real difference.

Things that help

  • Let them share at their own pace. Simply being there matters.
  • Believe them and listen without judgment.
  • Help them feel safe and in control, for example by offering choices.
  • Learn what triggers them and help reduce unnecessary reminders.
  • Encourage trauma-focused support, such as trauma-focused CBT or EMDR therapy.

Things to avoid

  • Pushing for details of what happened.
  • Saying "you should be over it by now".
  • Blaming them, or asking why they didn't act differently.
  • Making decisions for them without asking.

When to get professional help

Reach out to a professional, for yourself or someone you care about, if:

  • Distressing symptoms have lasted more than a month
  • Flashbacks, nightmares or anxiety are getting worse
  • It's hard to manage school, work or relationships
  • You're using alcohol or drugs to cope
  • There are any thoughts of self-harm or suicide (get help straight away)

A good first step is to talk to a doctor, psychologist, psychiatrist, or your school or workplace guidance counsellor. You can also browse support resources.

If someone is in immediate danger or at risk of harming themselves, don't wait: get urgent support now.

Look after yourself too. Supporting someone can be tiring. Make time to rest, set limits on what you can give, and lean on your own support network.
