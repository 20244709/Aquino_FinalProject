Understand mental health / Eating disorders

Eating disorders

What eating disorders are, the signs to look out for, and how to support someone who is living with one.

Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions involving persistent disturbances in eating behaviours and related thoughts and emotions. They can affect people of any age, gender, or body size, and recovery is possible with the right support.

Types of eating disorders

Eating disorders take several forms, and a person's experience can change over time.

  • Anorexia nervosa: Involves restricting food intake, an intense fear of gaining weight, and a distorted view of one's body weight or shape.
  • Bulimia nervosa: Involves repeated episodes of binge eating followed by behaviours to compensate, such as vomiting, misusing laxatives, fasting, or excessive exercise.
  • Binge eating disorder: Involves regular episodes of eating large amounts of food with a sense of loss of control, followed by distress, without regular compensatory behaviours.
  • ARFID: Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder involves limiting the amount or variety of food eaten, not because of body image concerns but due to sensory issues, low interest in eating, or fear of negative consequences.

Signs and symptoms

Eating disorders are often kept hidden, so changes in mood and behaviour can be the first things people notice.

Thoughts and feelings

  • Constant focus on food, weight or body shape
  • Intense fear of gaining weight
  • Strong guilt or shame around eating
  • Self-worth closely tied to appearance

Behaviours

  • Skipping meals, or strict rules about what and when to eat
  • Eating in secret, or avoiding eating with others
  • Going to the bathroom straight after meals
  • Exercising excessively, even when unwell or injured

Physical signs

  • Noticeable changes in weight, up or down
  • Tiredness, dizziness or fainting
  • Often feeling cold
  • Irregular or missed periods

Eating disorders affect people of every shape and size. You can't tell how unwell someone is from how they look.

Having some of these signs doesn't mean someone has this condition. Only a qualified professional can make a diagnosis.

How to help

You don't need to have all the answers. Being patient, kind and steady can make a real difference.

Things that help

  • Choose a calm, private time to share your concerns.
  • Focus on their feelings and wellbeing, not on food or weight.
  • Use "I" statements: "I've noticed you seem stressed at mealtimes, and I'm worried about you."
  • Keep mealtimes relaxed and keep including them in everyday activities.
  • Encourage professional help early. Eating disorders can seriously affect physical health.

Things to avoid

  • Commenting on their weight, body or what they eat, even as praise.
  • Making food the focus of every conversation.
  • Talking about dieting, "good" and "bad" foods, or your own body.
  • Expecting them to "just eat normally". Recovery takes time.

When to get professional help

Reach out to a professional, for yourself or someone you care about, if:

  • Thoughts about food, weight or body shape are taking over daily life
  • Regularly skipping meals, bingeing or purging
  • There are physical signs like dizziness, fainting or rapid weight change (see a doctor promptly)
  • They're withdrawing from friends, family or shared meals

A good first step is to talk to a doctor, psychologist, psychiatrist, or your school or workplace guidance counsellor. You can also browse support resources.

If someone is in immediate danger or at risk of harming themselves, don't wait: get urgent support now.

Look after yourself too. Supporting someone can be tiring. Make time to rest, set limits on what you can give, and lean on your own support network.
